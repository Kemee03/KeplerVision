The science · Transit method

A planet can reveal itself
by taking a little light.

Kepler watched stars for repeated dips in brightness. A transit is one possible explanation; follow-up and scientific vetting are still needed.

THE SIGNAL

A small shadow.
A measured change.

When a planet passes in front of its star from our viewpoint, it blocks a small fraction of the starlight. A telescope records brightness over time as a light curve. Repeated dips at regular intervals are a useful clue.

DepthHow far brightness falls
DurationHow long a dip lasts
PeriodTime between transits
FROM TELESCOPE SIGNAL TO MODEL OUTPUT

How Kepler Vision processes a curve

Each stage should be inspectable and reproducible.

01Input

3,197 ordered flux measurements describe one light curve from the labeled Kepler data.

02Scale

A scaler learned from training data puts cadence features on a comparable scale.

03Screen

AstroLens uses parallel 1D convolutions to examine patterns at different widths.

04Review

The model returns a probability. A flag is a candidate for inspection, not confirmation.

i

A transit-like dip is not proof of a planet. Instrument effects and other astrophysical sources can produce similar signals. Kepler Vision is a learning and engineering prototype, not expert vetting.

ARCHITECTURE

Multiple scales, one curve

Parallel 1D convolution branches use kernel widths 3, 5, 11, and 21. Their outputs are concatenated, pooled, then passed through a dense layer and sigmoid probability output.

k=3k=5k=11k=21→ Planet probability
TRAINING SETUP

Imbalance-aware training

Training data has 37 planet examples and 5,050 no-planet examples. AstroLens scales using training data and oversamples the minority class in training only.

No planet 5,050Planet 37
!
Do not use accuracy alone.

With five planets in the test split, recall moves in 20-point jumps. AstroLens currently misses four of them. This is a baseline, not a production-ready detector.
